Last semester, I endured the struggle of Linear Algebra for Biomedical Engineers, or something of that nature; I forgot what it was called. Though it was difficult, I learned a lot. My favorite part was learning about eigenvalues and eigenvectors. They have such a sophisticated, nice ring to their names.

In the spirit of Halloween, I couldn’t help but discuss a particular horror story that came from this class. At the beginning of the semester, the professor announced that he’d give extra credit to all the youngins who attended a one-on-one discussion with him during office hours. Instantly, I went from slouching to sitting upright, and geeked to locked in. Do you know how rare extra credit is in college, let alone engineering? To say the least, I was overjoyed. But do you know what the scary part was? We only had one midterm…

One midterm in a class is a double-edged sword. Traditionally, courses have three midterms, and the benefits include two other chances to bounce back from a bad grade. With one midterm, well, you only get one chance. The upside is that you only have to worry once, and then it’s all over. Up until the final, of course.

Now get this. The midterm was set to allow unlimited open notes. The professor himself said you could bring the whole textbook if you pleased. So I made the executive decision of simply not studying for the midterm. What’s the worst that could happen? If you know anything about open-note engineering exams, you know they’re like boxing a chimpanzee. You think you can win, but then it just rips your face off and throws it back at you. I ended up getting a really bad grade on the midterm, and I don’t even remember the score. All I know is that it wasn’t good.

After seeing my grade, I was about to tweak because I had just messed up my only opportunity to get an A in the class. My day was absolutely ruined, and my disappointment was immeasurable. Then I remembered.

The extra credit (EC).

I was saved. All I needed to do was ace this interview (the EC was an interview) with the professor, and maybe I’d get enough EC on the exam to make an A possible. I was already dreaming about making up for some lost points on the exam, and since it was worth 25% of my grade, that would boost my grade tremendously. But then I realized I didn’t really know much about the EC. I didn’t know if it was hard to receive the EC or how much I’d even receive if I nailed the interview. Then, the following lecture cleared things up.

“Guys, the EC replaces ONE homework grade.”

My head exploded. I tucked my A into bed and kissed it goodbye. Here I was, thinking I’d receive EC on the exam, only to find out it was on a lowly homework grade. Either way, I needed whatever I could get, so I signed up for the EC interview.

Towards the end of the semester, the EC interview came. I was quite nervous, especially because I hadn’t ever talked one-on-one with a professor. In fact, I don’t really talk much in general. Also, I’ve realized that I slur my words, so half the time, people don’t even understand what I’m saying. But on that interview day, I was like, “You know what? Today is the day I release the filter.”

I ended up doing well on the linear algebra aspect of the interview, but the real part of the horror story is when the professor asked, “Anything else?” and I took this as an invitation to start spewing bologna for bologna’s sake. I posed the question, “Why is there something, rather than nothing?” This was completely irrelevant to Linear Algebra for Biomedical Engineers by the way. And the problem is that I followed this question up with the same folly I published on my website, which has many holes and inconsistencies. What’s more, I had half a helping of articulation and more than twice a helping of malarky rolling off my tongue. My brain was on low battery, and my mouth was fully charged. I knew this during the interview, too, but I decided that I was already too deep into the discussion to back out, so I just committed to going down with the ship of dignity. I knew the professor was looking at me sideways, too. Pardon my language, but he was definitely like, “What the fluff? How many hits did it take to reach the moon?” Too many to count. So now I’m sitting here, at 3 a.m., cringed out by my decision to be stupid on purpose.

But that’s not what we came here for. We came here to answer the question, “Why is there something, rather than nothing?” Well, compared to my other answer, I think the more defensible argument is that something exists because reality has no temporal beginning. That, or reality has a beginning that does not involve a transition from nothingness. To be fair, I wasn’t completely on bull with my previous answer. I feel that there were some valid grounds, but I just didn’t defend them well.

I wish I had someone to talk to about this, but I don’t know anyone who really cares. I think you have to be in an existentialist trance to even begin to wonder what’s going on behind the scenes.
